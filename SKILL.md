# VCAA Text Response Style

Produce a defensible reading of the set text, not a polished university article and not a memorised theme essay. Emulate the decision-making visible in high-scoring VCAA work; never copy a candidate's wording or manufacture errors to resemble a student.

## Required references

- Read [references/vcaa-style-guide.md](references/vcaa-style-guide.md) for every task.
- For *Sunset Boulevard*, also read [references/sunset-boulevard-core-evidence.md](references/sunset-boulevard-core-evidence.md).
- Treat a user-supplied draft or voice sample as the authority for the student's vocabulary and sentence range.

This skill is for analytical text response. Do not apply it to Creating Texts or argument analysis unless the user explicitly asks to transfer one of its style principles.

## Choose the register

Default to **EAL** when the course is not stated but the surrounding project or request is EAL.

- **EAL:** favour direct claims, visible topic control, familiar evidence and sentences whose meaning remains clear on first reading. Correct errors, but do not inflate the prose beyond a plausible Year 12 student's voice.
- **English:** allow denser conceptual links and more compressed analysis only when they remain precise. Complexity must come from the reading of the text, not from thesaurus vocabulary.

In both modes, use English-style conceptual organisation with EAL-style clarity.

## Build the response

1. Parse the topic before selecting evidence.
   - Identify every concept, relationship, modifier and absolute term.
   - Identify the instruction: *how*, *discuss*, *do you agree?* or *to what extent?*
   - Restate the actual question in plain language without weakening it.
2. Form a one-sentence position that answers the exact degree or relationship in the topic.
   - Do not treat the proposition as something that simply needs to be proved.
   - Include a qualification only when the text genuinely supports one.
3. Design concept-led arguments.
   - Organise by ideas and their development, not one character per paragraph.
   - Give each paragraph a distinct job: establish, deepen, complicate or qualify the contention.
   - Make the order meaningful rather than relying on *Firstly / Secondly / Finally*.
4. Select central evidence.
   - Prefer major scenes, relationships, recurring motifs and the text's broad structure.
   - Draw evidence from different parts of the text.
   - For a film, use visible or audible details and no more metalanguage than the analysis needs.
   - Never invent wording, framing, lighting, props, sound or symbolism. Paraphrase when an exact quotation is uncertain.
5. Draft with an evidence-to-meaning chain:

   `specific moment -> relevant authorial choice -> meaning created -> implication for the topic`

   Give only enough plot context to locate the moment. Connect evidence clusters instead of presenting a list of examples.
6. Resolve the topic.
   - The conclusion should state what the text finally affirms, criticises or leaves unresolved and answer the required degree.
   - Do not merely rewrite the introduction or introduce new evidence.

## Natural exam voice

- Begin directly with the text, creator and position. Do not use a universal hook, dictionary definition or “This essay will argue”.
- Prefer concrete subjects and ordinary analytical verbs: *shows, depicts, frames, contrasts, links, reveals, suggests, exposes*.
- Repeat the creator's name and key topic terms when that is clearer than forced synonyms.
- Mix short declarative sentences with moderately complex ones. Let sentence length follow the idea.
- Use short embedded quotations. A quotation must provide language, irony or contrast to analyse; it is not proof that the plot occurred.
- Add context only where it changes the interpretation of evidence. Do not insert a detached history paragraph.
- Do not force every paragraph into an identical TEEL rhythm. The logic should be clear even if the paragraph shapes vary.

## Anti-AI gate

Remove or rewrite prose that has any of these patterns:

- ornamental praise such as *masterfully crafts*, *profoundly underscores* or *poignant reminder*
- vague abstraction such as *the multifaceted nature of the human condition*
- repeated rhetorical triads, repeated *not only ... but also*, or a mechanically balanced three-part thesis
- transition words that substitute for a real logical link
- strings of abstract nouns or film terms with no concrete scene attached
- claims that an isolated prop or subtle shot explains the entire film
- automatic claims about how “the audience is positioned” when audience response is not part of the argument
- identical paragraph openings and closings
- a sentence that sounds impressive but cannot be paraphrased plainly

Do not deliberately add grammar mistakes. A human exam voice comes from proportion, directness, useful repetition and uneven but purposeful rhythm, not fake imperfection.

## Final checks

- Every paragraph addresses the full topic, including its modifiers and absolutes.
- In a full essay or plan, the arguments are genuinely different and form a progression; in a standalone paragraph, the paragraph has one distinct conceptual job.
- Evidence is recognisable, verifiable and drawn from more than one part of the text.
- Every named technique is followed by an explanation of the meaning it creates.
- No paragraph is mainly plot summary, character biography or context dump.
- Sophisticated vocabulary earns its place by expressing a precise distinction.
- The response sounds plausible under timed Year 12 conditions and remains in the student's demonstrated voice.

Return only the form requested. A full essay should not contain headings, planning notes, VCAA commentary or citations unless the user asks for them.
