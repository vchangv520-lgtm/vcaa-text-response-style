# VCAA grounded style guide

## Source basis and limit

This guide was distilled on 22 September 2026 from official VCAA English and EAL external assessment reports for 2023, 2024 and 2025, with the 2026 NHT EAL report used as a later consistency check.

The latest completed local English/EAL pair is 2025. The 2026 Victorian main examinations had not yet occurred, so there was no 2026 English/EAL pair to compare. The 2025 EAL report contains a complete high-scoring *Sunset Boulevard* response to the loss-of-control topic. The 2025 English report contains cross-text high-scoring excerpts and specific advice about both *Sunset Boulevard* topics; it does not contain a second complete response to that same topic. In 2023, the English and EAL *Sunset Boulevard* topics tested parallel ideas but used different wording. Do not claim that VCAA published two complete same-topic *Sunset Boulevard* essays.

VCAA states that reproduced student work is not corrected for grammar, spelling or factual information and that samples are not automatically exemplary or complete. Treat the reports as evidence of rewarded decisions, not prose to copy.

## What the reports repeatedly reward

### Exact topic reading

Read the relationship between all of the topic's words. Modifiers such as *both, most, merely, whole, unavoidable, infatuation* and *to what extent* are not decoration. A response that discusses a nearby theme but ignores the modifier has changed the task.

### A reading rather than a list

The essay should interpret the text's ideas and values. Characters and events substantiate that reading; they should not determine the paragraph structure by default. Three character profiles do not become an argument merely because they appear in three paragraphs.

### Strategic progression

Paragraphs should do different conceptual work and make their relationship visible. Common useful movements are cause to consequence, appearance to underlying reality, personal choice to systemic pressure, or broad agreement to a precise limit.

### Selective, distributed evidence

High-scoring work uses evidence from several parts of the text and connects textual construction to meaning. For film, a central scene plus one accurate feature of framing, sound, performance, editing, lighting or mise-en-scène is stronger than a list of technical labels.

### Resolution

A conclusion should resolve the proposition's degree or tension. Summary is acceptable, but the stronger ending explains the judgement produced by the argument.

### Communication before display

VCAA's English report explicitly distinguishes purposeful sophisticated language from polysyllabic display. EAL samples show that occasional language errors do not erase strong topic control, interpretation and evidence. Neither point licenses careless writing: the goal is clear, accurate meaning.

## Observable exam style

- Openings are usually compact and answer the topic quickly.
- Key topic words recur naturally instead of being replaced by strained synonyms.
- Paragraphs often join two familiar moments into one idea.
- Transitions are functional and sometimes plain. Their value comes from the logical turn they name.
- Sentence polish varies because the work is a timed first draft. Do not imitate errors, but do not erase every trace of a student's natural rhythm.
- Film terminology is selective. The analysis spends more space on what a choice means than on naming the choice.
- Conclusions are short relative to body paragraphs.

The complete 2025 EAL *Sunset Boulevard* sample is about 900 words: a brief introduction, three substantial conceptual body paragraphs and a brief conclusion. This is an observation, not a mandated template or word count.

## EAL and English calibration

| Feature | EAL default | English default |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main priority | Clear analysis that conveys meaning | Coherent reading with fluent, precise expression |
| Topic handling | State the response visibly and return to key terms | Explore implications and the relationship between concepts |
| Syntax | Mostly direct, with manageable complex sentences | May be more compressed when the logic remains clear |
| Vocabulary | Familiar analytical verbs and precise topic language | Broader vocabulary only where it sharpens a distinction |
| Evidence | Recognisable scenes, short quotes, explicit meaning | Same foundation, with more conceptual compression where useful |

Best combined rule: use English-style conceptual organisation and EAL-style sentence clarity.

## Paragraph engine

Use this as a reasoning sequence, not a fill-in-the-blanks formula:

1. Make a claim that changes or advances the overall answer.
2. Locate a major moment with minimal plot summary.
3. Identify the authorial choice that matters.
4. Explain the meaning created by the interaction of detail and choice.
5. Connect a second moment that develops, tests or complicates that meaning.
6. Close on the paragraph's contribution to the exact topic.

If the topic asks for extent, the final body paragraph may offer a genuine limit. It should not be a token “however” paragraph that contradicts the rest of the essay without changing the final judgement.

## Anti-AI revision test

Revise any sentence that fails one of these tests:

- **Plain-language test:** can its claim be stated in ordinary English?
- **Scene test:** is the abstraction anchored in something verifiable in the text?
- **Why test:** does naming the technique lead to an explanation of meaning?
- **Topic test:** does the sentence answer this topic rather than any *Sunset Boulevard* topic?
- **Voice test:** is this vocabulary plausible beside the student's other sentences?
- **Necessity test:** would deleting the adjective, transition or context sentence change the argument?

Common default-LLM phrases to avoid unless the specific sentence truly needs them include *masterfully crafts, serves as a poignant reminder, multifaceted tapestry, profoundly underscores, delves into, through the strategic deployment of,* and *a scathing indictment of*. The problem is not that any one phrase is forbidden; it is that they often substitute tone for analysis.
